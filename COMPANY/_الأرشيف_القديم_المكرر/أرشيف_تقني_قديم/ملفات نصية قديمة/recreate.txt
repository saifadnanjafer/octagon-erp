# 1. WORKPLAN AND APPROACH (20 points)

## 1.1 Context (350 words max)

Basrah is one of the most climate-stressed cities on earth. Summer temperatures regularly cross 50°C, dust storms have become routine, and the canals that once gave the city its character now carry plastic instead of fish. Iraq generates millions of tons of municipal waste each year, and the majority of it ends up in informal landfills or open burns — much of it plastic, almost none of it formally recycled.

At the same time, Iraq imports nearly all of its consumer goods. Walk into any Iraqi home and the chairs, tables, lamps, and shelves on display were almost certainly made elsewhere. The country sends its waste to landfills and pays foreign currency for the goods it could be making from that same waste — two halves of a problem that have never been put in conversation.

Underneath this is a youth crisis. Iraq is one of the youngest countries on earth — over 60% of the population is under 30 — and youth unemployment in Basrah runs at roughly one in four. Young people with technical skills and ideas often leave or sit idle, because the local economy offers them few real pathways into modern manufacturing or design.

And there is a quieter crisis below all of this: the city's informal waste collectors, who already do the unseen work of pulling plastic out of the waste stream, do it with no recognition, no equipment, no safety, and no fair price for what they recover.

The root causes are connected. There is no local infrastructure to process plastic at scale; there is no cultural perception of waste as a resource; there is almost no domestic furniture-manufacturing sector to absorb recycled materials; and there is no visible model showing residents that what they throw away can come back as something they would actually want in their homes. Each of these gaps reinforces the others — and breaking one of them, in a way people can see and walk into, is the opening that makes change possible.

## 1.3 Your Solution (350 words max)

Recycling in Iraq today means landfills. Furniture in Iraq today means imports. We're proving neither has to stay true.

We're opening Basrah's first design-led recycled-furniture showroom inside our 11-year-old engineering workshop. Residents bring us their plastic waste and earn it back kilo-for-kilo as credit toward Iraqi-made chairs, tables, lamps, and modular shelving — all priced by weight, all designed and built by our young Iraqi team. A strong, active partnership with Basrah Municipality helps us collect and transform up to half a ton of plastic every month.

This addresses each layer of the problem above:

**The waste layer** — by giving plastic a second life that ends in someone's living room rather than a landfill, and by giving residents a reason to bring it to us instead of throwing it away.

**The imports layer** — by replacing pieces that would otherwise be shipped in from Turkey, China, or the Gulf with locally-designed Iraqi furniture.

**The youth layer** — by employing 3–4 young Iraqis full-time across collection, sorting, production, and showroom operations, with apprenticeship pathways into modern fabrication trades.

**The dignity layer** — by integrating Basrah's existing waste collectors into a transparent supply chain that pays fairly and acknowledges their work.

**The visibility layer** — by making the showroom itself the proof. People can walk in, sit on a chair, understand where it came from, and see the loop close in front of them. Climate action becomes something tangible, not abstract.

The $30,000 funds the pieces we don't already own: a plastic recycling line (shredder, extruder, compression press, and a large-format furniture 3D printer), plus the showroom buildout. The equipment we already run — a 1500W fiber laser, large-format CNC, 120×80 cm industrial 3D printer, and multiple CO2 lasers — becomes the unfair advantage that lifts our work out of the "rough recycled" category into designer quality.

When someone walks out carrying one of our chairs, they're carrying home the same plastic they once carried in.

## 1.4 Three Key Learning Questions (200 words max)

We see this project as much an experiment as a business. There are three questions we genuinely don't yet know the answer to — and the answers will matter to anyone trying this work in our region.

**1. Will a kilo-for-kilo exchange model drive deeper resident participation than cash-based buyback systems?** Most plastic-collection schemes pay people directly. We're betting that a system where waste comes back as something beautiful — something you can sit on, eat at, and show your guests — creates stronger emotional buy-in. We will test this directly by tracking enrollment, repeat exchanges, and per-household volume over time.

**2. Which categories of recycled furniture cross the cultural threshold from "compromise" to "desirable" in Iraq?** We don't yet know whether Iraqi customers will respond more strongly to chairs, lighting, modular storage, or architectural pieces. We will track sales by category and refine our product lines around what the market actually wants.

**3. Can high-end fabrication equipment shift the perception of recycled goods?** Most recycled products carry a "rough" aesthetic. With laser, CNC, and 3D printing in the loop, we want to find out whether design quality alone can change how recycled materials are valued — and at what price points.

## 1.7 Risk Framework (300 words max)

**Social & Environmental:**
*Risk:* Low initial resident participation, or contaminated/mixed plastic streams that complicate processing.
*Mitigation:* Awareness campaigns through schools, mosques, and the municipality; sorting station at intake with friendly guidance; transparent kilo-for-kilo system that builds trust quickly. Environmental risks from processing (fumes, microplastics) addressed through proper ventilation and closed-loop water systems.

**Financial:**
*Risk:* Equipment costs higher than projected, or furniture sales slower than expected in Year 1.
*Mitigation:* 10% contingency built into the budget; phased equipment procurement; existing workshop revenue from current operations cushions cash flow during ramp-up.

**Operational:**
*Risk:* Equipment failure or delayed delivery of imported components.
*Mitigation:* Our team can fabricate spare parts and many components in-house using our existing fiber laser, CNC, and 3D printers. We will source machines with regional service support where possible.

**Organizational:**
*Risk:* Team turnover, knowledge gaps, or scaling pains in design, sales, or production.
*Mitigation:* Cross-training across roles; documented standard operating procedures (we already run an established workflow system in our 11-year-old workshop); an apprenticeship layer that builds depth on each function.

**Political:**
*Risk:* Changes in municipal leadership or regulatory shifts affecting the partnership.
*Mitigation:* Build relationships across multiple municipal departments rather than depending on a single contact; design the model so it can function with or without municipal support.

**Strategic:**
*Risk:* The market doesn't accept recycled furniture at design prices.
*Mitigation:* Pricing flexibility (weight-based pricing scales naturally across budgets); diverse product lines; rapid iteration based on customer feedback; the showroom itself is a strategic asset that builds the brand.

**Safety & Security:**
*Risk:* Plastic processing involves heat, fumes, and cutting equipment.
*Mitigation:* Proper ventilation, PPE protocols, fire-safety equipment, staff safety training, and compliance with local industrial safety standards. Our 11-year workshop track record includes zero serious safety incidents.

# 2. INNOVATION (20 points)

## 2.1 What Makes Your Solution Innovative? (300 words max)

Most plastic recycling projects optimize for tonnage. Most furniture brands optimize for design. Almost no one is doing both — and no one in Iraq is doing both inside one walk-in space.

The innovation here is not in any single technology. Shredders, extruders, 3D printers, and CNCs all exist. The innovation is in the **combination, the location, and the loop**.

**1. The closed civic loop — kilo-for-kilo exchange.** Residents bring plastic. They earn credit by weight. They redeem that credit, by weight, on furniture made from the same kind of material they brought in. There's no abstract "tonnage" or vague climate promise — there's a chair, and someone made it from your bag, and the math is as simple as a scale. We have studied buyback systems used elsewhere (cash payments, points apps, deposit returns), and we believe the kilo-for-kilo exchange is uniquely suited to Iraqi context: it's tangible, it requires no smartphones, and it ends in something the household actually wants. To our knowledge, no recycled-furniture project in our region has built around this exact mechanic.

**2. Design-grade fabrication meets recycling.** We have learned from the Precious Plastic global community, which has shown that small-scale plastic recycling is technically viable in dozens of countries. Our addition is the design-grade fabrication layer (laser, CNC, large-format 3D printing) that transforms recycled material from a rough commodity into desirable design pieces.

**3. The showroom as proof.** Recycling is invisible by default. Our showroom makes it visible. You see the intake bin near the door, the workshop through a window, and the chair you're sitting on. This collapses the distance between waste, work, and product into one space.

**4. A local circular economy, not a single project.** Youth + waste collectors + municipality + residents in one self-sustaining loop, designed for replication.

## 3.5 Environmental & Climate Indicators (300 words max)

The clearest environmental indicator we will move is the volume of plastic diverted from Basrah's waste stream. At full operational scale (500 kg/month), we will divert 6 tons of plastic per year from landfills, canals, and open burning sites. Over the 24-month project period, that's at least 12 tons — and the model is built to keep growing past that.

That diversion translates into measurable secondary effects:

**Avoided emissions.** Open burning of plastic, common in Iraq, releases CO2, dioxins, and black carbon. By preventing the burning of 12+ tons of plastic, we conservatively avoid 25–35 tons of CO2-equivalent emissions over the project period. This figure draws on UNEP's published emission factors for open plastic burning (~2.9 tons CO2e per ton of plastic burned).

**Reduced demand for virgin plastic and imported furniture.** Every chair we build from recycled material is a chair not made from new plastic and not shipped from another country. Imported furniture in Iraq travels 2,000–5,000 km on average; localizing even a small share moves transport-emission indicators meaningfully.

**Microplastic prevention.** Basrah's canals and the Shatt al-Arab waterway carry plastic into the Gulf, where it fragments into the marine food chain. Diverted plastic doesn't reach the water.

**Behavior shift.** Once residents experience plastic-as-currency, the "throw it away" reflex weakens. We track this through enrollment numbers and repeat-exchange rates.

**Evidence base.** We draw on the Precious Plastic global community's body of work, which has demonstrated small-scale plastic-to-product loops in dozens of countries. Our innovation — the design-grade fabrication layer plus showroom-as-proof — is tested individually in other industries (designer goods, retail) but not yet combined for recycled materials in Iraq.

**Possible negative consequences** (fumes during processing, microplastic shedding, energy consumption) are mitigated through closed ventilation, water-based capture, and reliance on the workshop's existing solar-supplemented electrical infrastructure.

Our project speaks to multiple Sustainable Development Goals — most strongly **SDG 12** (Responsible Consumption and Production), **SDG 8** (Decent Work and Economic Growth), **SDG 11** (Sustainable Cities and Communities), and **SDG 13** (Climate Action), with meaningful contributions to **SDG 5** (Gender Equality) and **SDG 10** (Reduced Inequalities).

# 5. REPLICABILITY AND SCALABILITY (10 points)

## 5.1 Scale & Financial Sustainability (300 words max)

**At scale**, this looks like a network of design-led recycled-furniture showrooms across Iraq — Baghdad, Mosul, Najaf, Erbil, Sulaymaniyah — each tied to a local engineering workshop, each run by local youth, each transforming its city's plastic into furniture for its city's homes.

**Three dimensions of scale, drawing on the Y4C framing:**

- **Scaling out** — replicating the showroom model in additional Iraqi cities, then potentially across the region.
- **Scaling up** — engaging with municipal procurement (schools, public spaces) and informing national waste-management policy through demonstrated proof.
- **Scaling deep** — shifting the cultural meaning of "recycled" in Iraq from compromise to desirable.

**Adaptations for other contexts.** Each city will tune the plastic mix to what flows locally; each will adjust product lines to local taste; cities with different climate stresses (Baghdad's heat, Mosul's reconstruction need) will emphasize different categories. The underlying mechanic — kilo-for-kilo exchange + design-grade fabrication + showroom-as-proof — is portable.

**Financial sustainability beyond Y4C funding** rests on furniture sales. By Month 18, the showroom is projected to be revenue-positive on operating costs, with margins covering equipment maintenance, salaries, plastic acquisition (zero, by design — plastic is "paid" with future product), and modest growth.

Beyond direct sales, three secondary revenue streams emerge: (1) custom commissions for businesses and municipal projects, (2) replication consulting for other Iraqi workshops adopting the model, and (3) wholesale of recycled material/components to other makers.

**The model is intentionally light to replicate.** Design-grade equipment (laser, CNC, 3D printers) already exists in many Iraqi workshops that currently use them for signage and one-off jobs. What's missing is the recycling line and the showroom concept. Once we prove this works in Basrah, we offer those workshops a documented playbook — equipment specs, sourcing logic, exchange-system mechanics, lessons learned.

We are building toward a small business that funds the next showroom — not toward continued grant dependence.
