# 1. WORKPLAN AND APPROACH

## 1.1 Context (350 words max)

Basrah is one of the hottest cities in the world. Summers cross 50°C, dust storms have become normal, and the canals that used to define our city now carry plastic instead of fish. Iraq throws away millions of tons of waste each year, and most of it ends up in landfills or open burns — a lot of it plastic, almost none of it actually recycled.

At the same time, almost everything in an Iraqi home is imported. The chairs, the tables, the lamps, the shelves — all shipped from somewhere else. We send our waste to the dump and pay foreign currency for goods we could be making here from that same waste. The two halves of the problem have never really been put together.

Then there's the youth side. Iraq is a young country, and Basrah is no different — most of us are under 30. But many of my friends — engineers, designers, architecture students like me — finish their studies and find no real place to use what they've learned, especially anything to do with materials, fabrication, or sustainability. People with skills sit idle, or leave.

And under all of that, there are the waste collectors. They already do the work of pulling plastic out of the trash every day, but no one really sees them. They work without proper equipment, without safety, and they get a bad price for what they recover.

These problems are connected. There is no real infrastructure to process plastic locally. There is no culture of seeing waste as something useful. There is almost no Iraqi furniture industry to absorb recycled materials. And there is no working bridge between young Iraqis trained in design and the workshops that could employ them.

That's the gap we want to break. The point is not just to recycle plastic — it's to put all four of these problems in the same room and start solving them together, in a way people can actually see.

## 1.4 Three Key Learning Questions (200 words max)

We are still learning, and there are three things we genuinely don't know the answer to yet.

**1. Which way of processing each type of plastic gives us the best result for the price?** Different plastics behave differently. We will try compression, injection, and 3D printing across different materials and see which combinations actually produce something Iraqi customers want to buy at a price that works.

**2. Will people really show up with their plastic if we offer points instead of cash?** Most recycling schemes either pay people directly or rely on charity. We are betting that earning credit toward something local and well-made is a stronger reason to participate. We need to find out if that's true, and we will track how many people come back a second and third time.

**3. Can good design change how Iraqis see recycled stuff?** Most recycled products here look rough. With our equipment and a real designer leading the work, we want to find out if design alone can shift what "recycled" means — and at what price points.

## 1.7 Risk Framework (300 words max)

**Social & Environmental:**
*Risk:* Low resident turnout at first, or the plastic we get is too mixed or contaminated.
*Mitigation:* Awareness through schools, mosques, and the municipality. A friendly sorting station at intake. The points system makes participation easy and visible. Processing risks (fumes, microplastics) are handled with proper ventilation and water-based capture.

**Financial:**
*Risk:* Equipment costs more than we expect, or sales start slowly.
*Mitigation:* 10% contingency in the budget. Buying equipment in stages. Our existing workshop is already earning, so we have a cushion during ramp-up.

**Operational:**
*Risk:* A machine breaks down, or imported parts are delayed.
*Mitigation:* We can make most spare parts ourselves using the laser, CNC, and 3D printers we already have. We will buy machines that have service support in the region where we can.

**Organizational:**
*Risk:* Someone leaves, or knowledge gets stuck with one person.
*Mitigation:* We cross-train across roles. Our existing workshop already runs on a documented workflow, so this isn't new for us. We add an apprenticeship layer to deepen each role.

**Political:**
*Risk:* Municipal leadership changes, or regulations shift.
*Mitigation:* We build the relationship across more than one department, not with a single person. The lab can keep running even if the municipal partnership pauses.

**Strategic:**
*Risk:* The market doesn't accept recycled products at design prices.
*Mitigation:* Different quality grades at different prices. A range of products, from furniture to smaller modular pieces. We listen to customers and iterate quickly. We sell through both B2B and direct channels.

**Safety & Security:**
*Risk:* Plastic processing involves heat, fumes, and cutting tools.
*Mitigation:* Ventilation, safety gear, fire-safety equipment, and team training. The 11-year workshop has zero serious safety incidents on its record.

# 2. INNOVATION

## 2.1 What Makes Your Solution Innovative? (300 words max)

Most plastic recycling projects in our region focus on tonnage. Most furniture brands focus on design. Almost no one is doing both — and no one in Iraq is doing both inside one engineering workshop that already runs industrial-grade equipment.

The new thing here isn't a single technology. Shredders, extruders, 3D printers, and CNCs all exist already. What's new is how we combine them, who's leading the work, and how we set the conditions.

**1. We treat plastic like a real material, not "trash."**
Different plastics behave differently. We sort them, mix them carefully, and use the right method for each — compression for panels, injection for small parts, 3D printing for bigger structural pieces. We've learned a lot from the Precious Plastic community, who have shown that small-scale recycling can work in many countries. What we add on top is the design side and the bigger machines we already run.

**2. The design comes from an architecture student.**
I think about furniture the way I think about buildings — how it stands, how it works in the heat, how someone actually uses it. That makes our products feel different from what a pure recycler would make. They are designed to last, to be taken apart, and to come back into the cycle later.

**3. We're being honest about phasing.**
Rather than promising a polished public showroom in year one, we are building the production system first and the public space second. That removes risk and lets us refine the system with real production data.

**4. A workshop, the municipality, residents, and waste collectors all in one local circular economy** — designed so other Iraqi cities can copy it.

## 3.5 Environmental & Climate Indicators (300 words max)

The clearest thing we will move is how much plastic stays out of Basrah's waste stream. At full speed (around 500 kg per month), we divert about 6 tons a year from landfills, canals, and open burning. Across the 24 months of this project, we plan to keep at least 10 tons out of those places — and the system is built to keep growing past that.

That diversion has knock-on effects:

**Avoided burning emissions.** Open burning of plastic is common in Iraq, and it releases CO₂, dioxins, and black carbon. Keeping more than 10 tons of plastic out of the burn piles prevents a real amount of greenhouse gas emissions. (We will use the published research on plastic burning to estimate this properly when we report.)

**Less new plastic and less imported furniture.** Every panel, table, or chair we make from recycled material is one not made from new plastic and not shipped from another country. Imported furniture in Iraq travels long distances by ship and truck — replacing even a small share of that cuts the transport footprint.

**Less microplastic reaching the water.** Basrah's canals and the Shatt al-Arab carry plastic into the Gulf, where it breaks down and ends up in the food chain. Plastic we capture doesn't reach the water.

**A change in habits.** The points system changes "throw it away" into "bring it in." We will measure that by enrollment numbers and how often people come back.

**Where our evidence comes from.** We've studied the Precious Plastic projects worldwide — many of them work. What's new in our case is the design discipline, our existing fabrication equipment, and the lab-first approach.

We will manage processing risks (fumes, microplastic shedding, energy use) with proper ventilation, water capture, and the workshop's existing power setup.

# 4. COMMUNITY IMPACT

## 4.1 Community Impact & Behavior Change (300 words max)

Our project lines up with several of the Sustainable Development Goals — especially the ones about responsible production, decent work, sustainable cities, climate action, gender equality, and reducing inequality.

The impact reaches a few groups, in different ways:

**Young Iraqis** are at the center. We employ 3–4 full-time, with apprenticeship pathways for more. In a city where one in four young people is unemployed, these aren't just jobs — they are entry points into modern manufacturing, design, and digital fabrication. We will actively bring young women onto the team, especially into design and product roles, in a sector that has mostly excluded them. The fact that I — a 21-year-old architecture student — am leading the design side is part of the message: this is a place where young creative people belong.

**Waste collectors** are the people who already do this work invisibly across Basrah. Many are migrants or come from marginalized communities. They work without protection, without recognition, and at unfair rates set by middlemen. We bring them into our supply chain directly, paying transparently — the same rate scale we use with residents.

**Residents** experience the shift through actually doing it. Throwing away becomes bringing in. The points system is something you can hold — you don't need an app, you don't need to read anything. We are not trying to lecture anyone about the climate. We are giving them a small, dignified reason to change a habit.

**The wider community** gets a working example. Even before the public showroom, we will host school visits, university tours, and open workshop days, so the lab itself starts working as a quiet classroom.

We believe people change through experience, not slogans. When a child sees their family's plastic come back as a lamp, that lasts.

## 4.2 Target Audiences & Community Engagement (300 words max)

**Residents of Basrah neighborhoods.** Households across income levels — plastic waste isn't tied to one class. We made the points system easy on purpose: no app, no minimum amount, no fees. A child can bring one bag and walk away with points. Lower-income families benefit especially, because they tend to accumulate more packaging waste and have less access to formal disposal.

**Young Iraqis (18–30), especially young women.** Trained directly on the team in recycling-line work, digital fabrication, and product development. We will actively recruit young women into design and product roles. Most are high-school or university level, mix of trade and design backgrounds, currently underemployed or studying without a real outlet to use what they've learned.

**Informal waste collectors of Basrah.** Often migrants from other governorates or from marginalized backgrounds. Currently working without protective equipment, without fair pay, and without legal recognition. They become formal supply partners, paid transparently on the same scale as residents.

**Customers (Phase 1 leans B2B).** Interior designers, small businesses, NGOs, contractors, and over time, municipal procurement (schools, public spaces). Direct consumer sales (B2C) grow during Phase 1 and become a primary channel once we open the public space in Phase 2.

**How we involve them.** Residents shape the model directly — we run informal listening sessions in the first three months, and ongoing quarterly conversations refine the products and the points system. Customer feedback comes from sales data and direct conversation. Waste collectors are consulted on rates and how the pickup actually works for them. The youth team co-iterates on every part — they aren't just executing what we decide; they own the trajectory with us.

Nothing here is built *for* the community. It's built *with* the people who will carry it forward.

# 5. REPLICABILITY AND SCALABILITY

## 5.1 Scale & Financial Sustainability (300 words max)

If this works in Basrah, we want it to work elsewhere. The vision is a network of design-led circular manufacturing labs across Iraq — Baghdad, Mosul, Najaf, Erbil, Sulaymaniyah — each tied to a local engineering workshop, each run by young people there, each turning their city's plastic into local products.

Scaling for us happens in three ways. First, by replicating the lab model in other Iraqi cities. Second, by working with municipal procurement (schools, public spaces) and showing that the model is serious enough to inform local policy. Third, by slowly changing what "recycled" means to ordinary Iraqis — making it something people want, not something they settle for — and creating an actual career path for young designers and engineers in this country.

Each city would adjust the plastic mix to whatever flows there, tune the products to local taste, and emphasize different categories — for example, Baghdad might lean more into heat-related products, Mosul into reconstruction-related ones. The core mechanic — points-based collection, multiple processing methods, design-led output — travels.

Financially, the lab will pay for itself by the end of the project period. By Month 18, it should be revenue-positive on operating costs, with margins covering equipment maintenance, salaries, plastic acquisition (which stays low because of the points system), and modest growth. Phase 2 (the public showroom) will be funded mostly by Phase 1 revenue, plus additional grants if available.

Three other revenue streams will emerge over time: custom commissions for businesses and municipal projects, replication consulting for other Iraqi workshops adopting the model, and wholesale of recycled material to other makers.

The model is intentionally light to copy. Many Iraqi workshops already own laser, CNC, or 3D printers. What's missing is the recycling line, the material discipline, and the design framing — and we will share all of it openly.

# 7. COMMUNICATION

## 7.1 Communication Strategy (300 words max)

The strongest message we have is the work itself — the products, the photos of plastic going from a bag to a chair, the team. Around that, we will speak to different people in their own language and on their own platforms.

**Residents of Basrah** are our main audience for the points system. The goal is to get them coming through the door with a bag of plastic, not just nodding at a poster. We use Instagram, TikTok, and Facebook to show the loop in action. We work through schools, universities, and mosques with the municipality. We rely on neighborhood word-of-mouth, and we put posters at municipal collection points. The message is simple: ***"Bring it in. Earn points. Take design home."***

**Young Iraqis (potential team and apprentices)** we reach through Instagram and through architecture, design, and engineering departments at the universities. We frame the work as creative, technical, and forward-looking — not "waste sorting." Message: ***"Help build the country's first design-led circular manufacturing lab."***

**Customers (designers, businesses, eventually households)** we reach through Instagram (good product photos and behind-the-scenes), local interior-design and lifestyle pages, and direct outreach to architects and contractors. Message: ***"Iraqi-made, locally produced, real design."***

**Policymakers, media, and the wider Y4C network** we reach with monthly impact updates, an annual public report, English-language posts to widen the reach, and direct relationships with municipal officials and local press. Message: ***"This is what local circular economy looks like when it is engineered, not improvised."***

We measure communication by what people do, not by how many like a post: how many residents enroll, how many products sell, how much press we get, how many other workshops ask to copy the model.

The plan keeps itself running because every batch we produce becomes new content.
